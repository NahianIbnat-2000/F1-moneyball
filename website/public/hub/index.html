F1 Moneyball

The Economics Behind the Speed

Formula 1 teams are firms. They raise money, hire engineers, and compete for a fixed set of prizes, and since 2021 a cost cap limits how much most of them can spend. F1 Moneyball uses public financial filings, race data and tourism statistics to answer three questions, each in its own study:

  1. Does money buy speed? Do richer teams score more points?
  2. Did the cost cap work? Did the 2021 spending limit make the sport more equal?
  3. Who else gains? Does hosting a Grand Prix bring more tourists to the region?

Each study is published as an article on the website. The first is also written up as a working paper.

Personal researchThis is my own independent project, done in my own time. It is not connected to any university, team, the FIA or Formula 1, and it has not been peer reviewed. All data and code are public so anyone can check the work.

Three sides of F1's economy: teams, rules, host regions

Three studies

One project looks at Formula 1 from three sides: the teams (does money go with results?), the rules (did the cost cap change the balance between teams?) and the regions that host races (do races bring tourists?). The studies share the race and regulation data and one pipeline, but each answers its own question with its own data, and none depends on another's results. Each is a research article on the website with interactive charts. Only Study 01 is also written up as a working paper (PDF).

01

Does Money Buy Speed?

Web articleWorking paper

Do richer teams score more points, and does a team do better after it spends more?

Audited accounts from UK Companies House filings for 8 team lineages, linked to championship results from 2010 to 2025. Panel models with team and season fixed effects, and a wild cluster bootstrap because there are few teams.

Finding: comparing different teams, richer teams score more points: money share and points share move closely together (correlation 0.78). Comparing each team with itself in its other seasons, the link is much weaker. The clearest result is for staff spending: 10% more goes with finishing about 0.4 places higher in the next season's championship. The link with points share is less certain once changes in how the accounts are drawn up are taken into account, and no within-team result holds after correcting for running several tests at once. With eight teams this is suggestive, not proof, and it shows things moving together, not that money caused the results.

02

The Cost Cap Paradox

Web article only

Did the 2021 cost cap bring the teams closer together?

Measures how evenly points are spread across teams, before and after the cap. To check whether the 2021 change is unusual, the same test is also run with fake start dates: pretending the cap began in other years when nothing changed (called placebo years). If those fake dates show changes just as large, the real one is not special. A second test compares teams the cap restricted more with teams it restricted less.

Finding: so far, the change after 2021 cannot be told apart from changes in placebo years. The cap and the 2022 technical rules came one season apart, so their effects cannot be separated.

No working paper yet. One may follow once more seasons under the cost cap are available; five seasons are too few to separate the cap from other changes.

03

The Grand Prix Economy

Web article only

Does hosting a race bring more tourists to the region?

Monthly tourist arrivals for two hosts where the race started or came back during the years with data: Austria (Styria, back on the calendar in 2014; data from 2008) and Singapore (first race 2008; data from 2005). Each host is compared with its own past, and Styria also with the rest of Austria. The two hosts are not compared with each other. Long-running hosts such as Hungary (a race every year since 1986) have no "before" to compare with. The analysis stops in 2019 to avoid COVID.

Finding: Styria's race month shows more arrivals than usual, but much less once compared with the rest of Austria, and the synthetic control does not separate it from chance. Singapore shows no clear race-month rise.

No working paper yet. One may follow if data for more host regions become available; two hosts are too few for a full paper.

Earlier research

What was already known, and what this adds

None of these questions is new. Other researchers have studied each of them, and my articles and working paper discuss their work. This is the main earlier research, and what my project adds to it.

StudyEarlier workWhat this project adds
01 · Does money buy speed? Loník and Kotrba (2024), in Managerial and Decision Economics, find that F1 budgets are linked to better standings, points and podiums over 2010 to 2020, before the cost cap. Money figures from audited company accounts, each traced to its filing. A split between comparing different teams and comparing each team with itself, which give very different answers. Methods built for only eight teams. Data that runs into the cost-cap years, 2021 to 2025.
02 · Did the cost cap work? Master's and senior theses (Foghsgaard and Nymand, 2024; Jung, 2023) find no clear change in competitive balance after 2021, and note that the 2022 rule change makes the cap hard to isolate. Placebo years, to check whether the 2021 change is unusual at all, and a comparison of teams the cap restricted more with teams it restricted less, using their audited spending before the cap.
03 · Who else gains? Ramasamy and Yeung (2020) find that hosting a Grand Prix raises a country's tourist arrivals by about 6% on average. Ramasamy, Wu and Yeung (2022) find the effect differs a lot from country to country. A regional view instead of whole countries: the host region compared with other regions of the same country, for races that started or came back during the data, so there is a clear before and after.
Method

How I worked

I only used figures I could check against an original source, and every dataset on the website links back to where it came from. The numbers in the paper are pulled from that same data by code, so the paper and the website always match. With eight teams and two race hosts, most results show things moving together rather than one causing the other, and I say that wherever it applies. The data, the code and the paper's LaTeX source are all on GitHub, so anyone can check or rerun the work.
